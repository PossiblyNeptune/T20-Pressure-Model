Pressure is how far the next ball can swing the result

Before every ball we estimate the chasing side's chance of winning from the score, wickets, balls left and target. The Leverage Index (LI) is how much the possible outcomes of the next ball would move that chance, scaled so an average ball scores 1.

A dead game scores close to 0, however high the required rate. A tight finish can reach 20 or more. That is why we do not use the required run rate on its own: it peaks at 900 an over in this data, in a match already lost, and it barely tracks LI at all (correlation −0.03).

Six steps turn every ball into a fair, comparable rating

Each step was added because a check on the data showed a bias without it. The maths sits under each step for anyone who wants it.

Rebuild the situation before every ball

From the raw deliveries we rebuild score, wickets, legal balls left and target for each of 200,999 balls. Totals reproduce the official result margins, so the ledger is sound.

Show the detail
Legal ball = not a wide or no-ball. Ball faced = every delivery except wides. Batter runs = runs off the bat. Bowler charged = bat runs + wides + no-balls. Bowler wicket = any dismissal except run out, retired out, obstruction. Rain-shortened games use the revised target and length. Super overs (38 balls) and no-result matches are set aside.

Turn the situation into a win chance and a pressure score

A win-probability model for chases, an exact calculation for the last 12 balls, and a first-innings model that values the total being built. LI follows from it.

Show the maths
Chase: gradient-boosted trees (runs needed, balls left, wickets in hand, required rate), with monotonic constraints. Held-out accuracy: Brier 0.131 against 0.250 for a coin flip, AUC 0.90. Last 12 balls: backward calculation from next-ball odds by ask-rate and wickets, recalibrated on real finishes. First innings: Brier 0.219, AUC 0.70.
LI = Σ p(outcome) × |WP after outcome − WP now| ÷ league average of the same

Score each ball against players in the same spot

Win probability added by the ball, divided by its LI, minus what an average player achieves in that innings, phase and pressure band. A powerplay single and a last-over six land on one scale.

Show the maths
score = WPA ÷ LI − league average(innings × phase × LI band)
Batter gets bat runs and his own dismissal; bowler gets runs charged and his wickets. Single-ball values capped at ±8. Balls with LI below 0.1 (result decided) are excluded.

Correct for who, where and when

Opposition strength by team and year, ICC tier, ground, and season scoring levels are all removed. Players who play both IPL and T20Is put the two competitions on one scale.

Show the maths
Ridge regression on ball scores with batting team-year, bowling team-year, tier (Full Member, Associate, A-team) and venue. Associates sit about 0.13 below Full Members (39 head-to-head games). Each competition-year is centred on its own average, removing IPL scoring inflation from 2022 to 2026. The IPL–T20I level is set from 48–51 players who appear in both; afterwards their IPL and T20I levels match within 0.007.

Combine usual level with the pressure gap, trusted only as far as it repeats

Pressure-only samples are small, so a player's rating is his usual level plus his pressure gap, each shrunk by how repeatable it proved in split-half tests.

Show the maths
rating = u × τu²/(τu²+SEu²) + c × τc²/(τc²+SEc²)
u = average ball score; c = score on above-normal-pressure balls (weight LI − 1, capped at LI 5) minus u. True spread of usual level τu: 0.071 batters, 0.049 bowlers. True spread of the pressure gap τc, from 16 random split-halves of the matches: 0.052 and 0.048. A typical player's gap is trusted about 15–18%.

Rank by how surely a player beats a replacement in his role

The Reliable Pressure Margin: the 90% lower bound of a player's rating above a fringe player in the same role. Proven players beat short hot streaks, and each batter is judged against a replacement in his own role: top order, middle order or finisher.

Show the maths
margin = (rating − role replacement) − 1.28 × posterior SD
Replacement = pooled pressure performance of fringe players (60–299 balls) in the same batting role (top order 1–3, middle order 4–5, finishers 6–7, lower order), or bowling phase × pace/spin, averaged over the balls each player actually faced. Eligibility: 300+ live balls and 30+ balls at LI 2 or more.

How we got here: eight checks that changed the method

Every version of the ranking was tested against the data and against cricket sense. These are the problems those tests caught, and what we changed.

Required run rate peaked at 900 an over

Raw RRR treats a lost cause as maximum pressure. We built a win-probability model and measured pressure as the swing the next ball can cause.

Rain-shortened games looked 684 balls long

The “revised overs” columns hold balls, not overs. We read them as balls; 48 shortened innings now have the right length.

The model gave “4 off the last ball” an 87% chance

Tree models smooth over the last-ball cliff; real teams win about a quarter of those. The last 12 balls of a chase are now calculated exactly and recalibrated on real finishes.

A UAE opener ranked 4th

Runs against associate attacks counted like runs against India. Teams are rated by year and shrunk toward their own ICC tier; associates sit 0.13 below Full Members.

2026 batters looked better than 2022 batters

IPL scoring inflation flattered recent batters and earlier bowlers. Every competition-year is centred on its own level, and players who appear in both bridge IPL and T20I.

Only 27% of the “pressure rating” came from pressure balls

Plain LI weighting was mostly measuring normal cricket. Pressure is now weighted by how far a ball exceeds normal, two-thirds from LI 2+ balls, and combined with usual level by measured reliability.

Bowlers' pressure gap looked twice its true size

A formula-based estimate put it at 0.116, promoting small samples. We measured it directly from split halves of the matches: 0.048.

Top order and finishers faced the same bar

Fringe middle-order batters are scarcer than fringe top-order batters. Each ball carries the replacement level of its role (top order, middle order, finisher), so a finisher is judged against a fringe finisher.
